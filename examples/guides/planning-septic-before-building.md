# Planning septic before you buy rural Texas land

> Educational sample using Out West Septic as the chosen example business. This is a planning guide, not a parcel evaluation, septic design, permit approval, or price offer. [Read the teaching notes](../../annotations/planning-septic-before-building.md).

A rural tract can have room for a house while leaving important wastewater questions unresolved. Before buying land or fixing the locations of the driveway, home, and outbuildings, find out what information a septic professional needs to evaluate the property. A useful early goal is a workable site plan with clearly recorded unknowns.

Start with the intended use of the land. Explain the home you want now, then identify possible additions: a guest house, workshop bathroom, or other occupied building. Present future ideas as proposals so the professionals reviewing the property can explain what each would require.

## Assemble a property file before requesting an evaluation

Gather the parcel identifier, county, available survey, and any existing site plans. Include the property address if one has been assigned; otherwise, provide a map pin and clear access directions. Keep measured boundaries separate from approximate lines drawn on an aerial image.

Add the proposed home layout and information about any existing septic system. Request available permits, plans, and maintenance records from the seller. Mark missing documents as unknown instead of assuming that an old tank or a seller's description establishes capacity for your intended use.

Ask the local permitting authority which records and planning materials apply to the parcel. TCEQ links to the permitting-authority lookup in its permit guide. Use the [permit questions guide](septic-permit-questions.md) to prepare that conversation. [TCEQ: Getting a Permit for an OSSF](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/permitting/ossf/ossfpermits.html).

## Use online soil information to prepare better questions

USDA's Web Soil Survey lets you define an area of interest, view mapped soils, and create a report. It is useful background for a property discussion. USDA also explains that soil surveys support planning and that some engineering uses need onsite investigation. A map report is not approval for a particular septic system. [USDA NRCS: Web Soil Survey](https://websoilsurvey.nrcs.usda.gov/app/).

Save the report with the parcel information and ask the evaluator what it helps explain, what needs checking on the ground, and whether the proposed disposal area warrants closer investigation. Do not select a system simply because a map names a soil type or a neighbor has a particular installation.

## Keep the first layout flexible

Prepare one sketch showing the house, driveway, parking, proposed well or water connection, outbuildings, visible drainage features, and areas you hope to preserve. Label future buildings distinctly. Ask the evaluator which additional features and measurements are needed; the sketch is a conversation aid rather than a construction plan.

For a planning exercise, imagine two possible home locations. One leaves the broad open area available for evaluation. The other places the driveway and a future workshop through it. Bring both options to the discussion before committing to either. Ask how each arrangement changes the areas available for the system and access for installation and future service.

TCEQ's system-selection guidance considers evaluated conditions including soil, groundwater, and restrictive layers. The site's actual findings matter when selecting a suitable system. [TCEQ: Choosing a Septic System](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/permitting/ossf/ossfsystems.html).

## Arrange the evaluation before committing to ground work

TCEQ states that a site must be evaluated by a licensed site evaluator or licensed professional engineer before septic construction or the other work covered by its guidance. Ask who will perform that role and what documentation you will receive. An informal discussion with an installer and the required evaluation are different steps. [TCEQ: Choosing a Septic System](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/permitting/ossf/ossfsystems.html).

Bring your preferred layout and alternatives to the evaluation. Ask which decisions can be made from the findings, which need design work, and which remain subject to the permitting authority. Before authorizing clearing, grading, or a pad location, have the relevant professionals identify what must remain undisturbed or adjustable while those questions are resolved.

## Leave with a written decision brief

Record the answers in a short document shared with the people arranging the build:

| Decision | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Intended use | Current home proposal and any future buildings discussed |
| Evaluated area | Location and date of the evaluation; person responsible |
| Layout | Features agreed in principle and positions still provisional |
| Open questions | Missing information, responsible person, and next action |
| Next commitment | What must be confirmed before ordering work or equipment |

If comparing parcels, use the same brief for each one. When the project is ready for pricing, carry that shared information into your [estimate comparison](compare-septic-estimates.md). A property with unanswered questions should retain those questions in your comparison; avoid turning a preliminary conversation into a definite installation allowance or completion date.

For the next stage, the [installation inquiry guide](../service/septic-installation.md) explains what to gather for a service discussion.

To discuss a property with the example business, contact [Out West Septic](https://outwestseptic.com/) with its location, your intended use, and the documents already available. Confirm service availability and scope for that specific project, and ask what information is needed for the next step.
